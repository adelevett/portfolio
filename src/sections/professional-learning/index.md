<h2 id="csun-2026">CSUN Assistive Technology Conference 2026</h2>

Attended the 41st CSUN Assistive Technology Conference sessions on assistive technology, accessible media, and emerging tools to inform my outreach and student support. Sessions I attended included:

- AI for You: Everyday Strategies to Lighten Your Cognitive Load (Presenter: Daiana Wheeler). This session reframed AI as a thought partner for reducing cognitive overload and decision fatigue, offering practical routines and prompts across planning, processing, decision support, communication, and reflection. The framework for protecting focus and clarity has implications for how I model sustainable workflows in my own practice and support students managing competing demands.

- Is AI Good for the DeafBlind Community? (Presenter: Bryan Ward). This session examined how emerging AI tools impact DeafBlind individuals in everyday life, from the accuracy and limitations of AI-powered captioning and automated image descriptions to the opportunities, biases, and ethical implications of AI in employment. The discussion sharpens my approach to evaluating AI-driven tools for the students we serve, ensuring that accuracy and agency—not novelty—drive adoption.

- Social-Haptic Communication for DeafBlind AT Instruction (Presenters: Bryan Ward, Tara Brown-Ogilvie). This session covered the practical application of haptics, a standardized system of touch signals on the body, to convey real-time environmental information, social feedback, and visual-spatial orientation during assistive technology instruction. Expanding my awareness of communication modalities beyond visual and auditory channels strengthens my ability to match instructional strategies to individual learner needs.

- Accessible Cybersecurity Education for Deaf or Blind Students (Presenters: Steven Forney, Jesse Hairston). This session outlined strategies and practical accommodations for immersive technology experiences for high school students who are deaf, hard of hearing, or visually impaired. The presenters offered a model for inclusive program design, highlighting 3D-printed tactile Caesar Cipher decoder rings with Braille and vertical lettering, tactile binary learning toggles, and APH accessible snap circuits. The emphasis on pre-program assistive technology surveys, standardized ASL vocabulary, and deaf and visually impaired role models sets a model for technology integration and equitable access.

- Physical and Motor Accessibility Solutions for iPhone (Presenter: Chris Fleizach). This session surveyed Apple's physical and motor accessibility tools across iOS, watchOS, and tvOS—Voice Control, Switch Control, Head Tracking, and AssistiveTouch—along with practical workarounds such as Confirm with AssistiveTouch for Apple Pay, Apple Watch Mirroring, Touch Accommodations for tremors, and Assistive Access. Hearing how AssistiveTouch grew out of an effort to connect an iPhone to a wheelchair joystick before becoming a universal accessibility feature was a powerful reminder that accommodations designed for disabled users frequently benefit everyone.

- Why This Image Here? An Education Driven Approach to Alt Text (Presenter: Laura Ciporen). This session presented a framework for writing and evaluating informative image descriptions by adopting an educational mindset that treats readers as learners. Distinguishing content, non-content, and decorative images, and guiding authorship with three questions (Why is this image here? How will it be used? What will the sighted audience gain?), directly informs the alt text and extended description practices in our outreach.

- AI Co-Design for Tactile Graphics: Potential, Pitfalls and Prompts (Presenters: Chancey Fleet, Lauren Race). This session explored the design of tactile graphics for blind and low-vision users and the current capabilities and limitations of AI models in generating tactile-ready graphics. The core design rules—scaling for tactual acuity, removing background clutter, replacing color with distinct line textures, and 36pt Braille labels—alongside the case study using AI and Python SVG scripts to produce tactile pie charts, inform both my approach to Non-visual Design with tactile SVG and my realistic expectations for AI assistance in tactile production.

- Continuous AI for Accessibility (Presenter: Ed Summers). This session showed how to integrate "Continuous AI" and GitHub Copilot into the software development lifecycle to scale digital accessibility, from custom repository instructions that enforce accessibility standards to automated pull request review and an open-source accessibility scanner that detects violations, files issues, and assigns fixes. The regulatory context around updated ADA Title II reinforces the urgency of embedding accessibility into daily practices rather than bolting it on after the fact.

- AI & The Future of Accessible Documents (Presenter: Ann Knettler). This session explored how AI tools support accessible PDF workflows in education, marketing, and technology settings, with a focus on integrating automation with expert review to ensure compliance with legal standards while delivering high-quality, user-friendly documents. The pairing of automated efficiency with human expertise mirrors our alternate media practice, where AI-assisted conversions still depend on skilled review to guarantee quality and compliance.

- Generative AI's Potential to Supporting Deficits in Reading Comprehension (Presenter: Lisa Sousa). This session explored using large language models as cognitive scaffolds for students with specific learning disabilities in inferential reading comprehension. The shift from AI as a shortcut to AI as a "thinking partner"—with students critiquing the model's step-by-step reasoning against textual evidence, and prompts fading over time to build independence—offers a thoughtful guardrail for how I introduce AI tools to students, keeping critical thinking and agency at the center.

- AI as an Accessibility Assistant: A Low-Vision Pipeline for STEM (Presenter: Wayne Dick). This session examined why STEM knowledge, though born digital, remains inaccessible to people with low vision—identifying the PDF as the culprit, since it treats whitespace as invisible and breaks reflow as words run together—and demonstrated transforming PDFs into a reflow-safe format. The pipeline offers a promising path toward making STEM materials genuinely usable for our students with low vision.

<h2 id="caped-2025">California Association for Postsecondary Education and Disability (CAPED) 2025</h2>

Participated in CAPED 2025 by contributing a presentation on artificial intelligence as it relates to assistive technology and alternate media services [Head in the Cloud?](/2025/fall/caped/). Sessions I attended addressed building a professional community, developing disability identity for student success, statewide accessibility
resources, and the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on equity in education.

- Building Your Assistive Technology Community: Strategies for Connection and Collaboration (Presenter: Mike Sauter) This session reinforced the importance of professional community as a foundation for equitable technology access. I participated in the strategy evaluation for building collaborative AT networks across institutions, with an emphasis on sustainable knowledge sharing and cross-campus support systems that align with my commitment to professional growth and student-first services. 

- Disability, Diagnosis, and Difference: Developing a Cohesive Disability Identity (Presenter: Lisa Yates). This presentation offered a social justice framework for understanding disability identity and language. The discussion around rejecting ableism and uniting across silos strengthens my approach to supporting students in developing technology literacy that honors disability as identity in direct support of student success. 

- What is HTCTU (Presenter: Stephen A. Marositz). This overview of the High Tech Center Training Unit (HTCTU) clarified statewide strategies for equitable access within the CCC system. Discovering the evolution of this statewide resource about current initiatives in alternate media, Section 504 compliance, and system-wide training informs how I align local practices with broader accessibility goals. 

- AI as an Accommodation: Are We Ready? (Presenters: Brian Krause, Mike Sauter, Laurie Vasquez, and Tiffanie Lau). This roundtable prompted critical reflection on the role of generative AI as a potential accommodation under disability services. The discussion on ethical, institutional, and policy implications directly informs my work evaluating AI’s role in student support—ensuring that emerging technologies expand, rather than replace, genuine access and agency for disabled learners. 

- Ethical AI Implementation - for Neurodivergent Student Success in Higher Education (Presenters: Bianca Hernandez and Cristina Montejano). This session explored frameworks for responsibly integrating AI into higher education, centering neurodivergent students’ experiences. The emphasis on bias, privacy, and authentic assessment connects directly to the college community's efforts to grapple with the role of AI in education. 

<h2 id="flex-days">Flex Days</h2>

Regular Flex Day presentations, including a recent session on [Accessibility Features 101](/2026/spring/twc/). Sessions I attended include:

- Against Alienation and Cynicism: Finding Joy and Meaning for All in College Classrooms (Presenter: Susan D. Blum | Facilitator: Sarah Fama). Blum’s work on Ungrading and the anthropology of learning reminds us that students—and educators—are first and foremost human learners. Her call to resist cynicism resonates deeply with my own work supporting students with disabilities, emphasizing that inclusive learning begins with curiosity, joy, and authentic engagement rather than compliance or accommodation alone. 

- No Course Fees and No Course Materials Costs: Utilizing Free Course Materials to Engage Your Classroom Community (Facilitator: Jennifer Howze-Owens). This session reinforced my interest in open educational resources (OER) as a means of day one access to learning materials. The focus on accessibility aligns with adapted technology practices prioritizing equitable access and has important implications for our Alternate Media Services. 

- Evaluating Open-Source ASR Systems: Performance Across Diverse Audio Conditions and Error Correction Methods (Authors: Saki Imai, Tahiya Chowdhury, Amanda Stent). Research in open-source speech recognition speaks directly to the challenges and possibilities in adapting emerging technologies for accessibility. This work underscores the importance of equity-minded evaluation—ensuring that tools used by disabled learners are not only functional but non-discriminatory across accent, gender, and environmental diversity. 

- Thinking with Images and its Role in Promotion of Equity (Presenter: Mohsen Janatpour | Facilitators: Darryl Stanford, Justin Stevick). This session highlighted the importance of fostering imagination and creativity as an equity practice. I have since translated insights from the session in my workshop on creating effective study notes from the summaries generated by users of our note-taking adapted technologies. 

- Creating an Equity-Centered Culture of Assessment at CSM (Facilitators: Natalie Alizaga, Allie Fasth, Daniel Keller). The focus on equity-centered assessment aligns closely with my ongoing efforts to ensure that technology tools and accommodations are evaluated not just for functionality but for inclusive impact. The discussion around shared responsibility for equity in assessment reinforced how accessibility and institutional culture must evolve together to truly support all learners. 

- Rethinking Assessment in the Age of AI (Presenter: Kelly Spoon). This workshop helped me understand the demands on faculty for maintaining equitable assessment strategies as AI tools have become ubiquitous. By thinking critically about the potential tension between educational goals and technological innovation I was able to reflect on my aspirations for student agency and how to support students engaging with AI.
